Oslo City Bike, measured against humanity-centered design.

August 29, 2026 · 6 min read

The Oslo City Bike scheme is a local bike-sharing service I am proud to have in my city. Oslo has become more sustainable over the years, welcoming more bike-friendly paths through the city, and that has drawn more people into an environmentally friendly service running some 3,000 bikes across 266 stations in the heart of Oslo. A rider gets an hour, and can extend by up to two more for a very small amount every fifteen minutes. Even in a city as hilly as this one, it is a cheap, eco-friendly way to get around.

The five principles this is measured against

  • 01 Focus on the ecosystem, not the single product.
  • 02 Solve the root issue, not the symptom.
  • 03 Take a long-term systems approach.
  • 04 Test and refine the design continuously.
  • 05 Work with the community, not for them.

What the scheme sets out to solve

  • The last-mile problem. When traffic jams build and other sudden challenges hit the busy city roads, the bikes fill a gap in Oslo’s public transport network.
  • Bike theft. This is a very common problem in Oslo. Every day there is at least one private bike to spot with vital components missing — still attached to a rack, or dumped in a river, with or without parts. Not everyone can store a bike in the city, and even a safe garage is a risk. The scheme lets people avoid the cost, the maintenance and the fear by removing ownership altogether.
  • Cars in the city centre. Fewer of them means wider bike pathways, which in turn makes for a safer, cleaner and more active urban environment. When I lived near the palace park, with a balcony facing a one-way driveway, I could easily see layers of pollution gathering on the railing.
  • Steep hills. Oslo is known for them, which makes it a challenging city to cycle. White electric bikes are included as an option that takes the physical exertion out of the climb.
  • Cost. Using the service is very cheap next to public transport or owning a car, and you can pick up and drop off almost anywhere in the city.

Who actually rides it

An estimated 55,000 unique active users take trips annually. Two groups stand out.

Daily commuters and workers. They hit delays in public transit and walk to the nearest docking station instead. They want fresh air on the way to the office, along Oslo’s friendly bike routes, but still quick enough for the morning coffee with colleagues. Some use it to travel between meetings in the city centre during the working day.

Residents inside the transit zones. I am one of them — living and working in zone 1 for almost thirteen years, a zone covering all of Oslo and inner municipalities like Bærum, including the entire metro system. I can say, hand on heart, that I have used these bikes for quick errands, shopping, short trips for fun, and socialising in central neighbourhoods and parks.

Measured against the five principles

01 — Focus on the ecosystem

The scheme has a well-designed structure and sits well inside Oslo’s transport ecosystem. It benefits the environment by reducing car use, congestion and emissions, and it makes clear how transport ties people, the city and the environment together as interconnected systems. That said, it would not be nearly as effective if Oslo had not invested heavily in bike lanes and cycling infrastructure first.

02 — Solve the root issue

The goal is to reduce dependence on private cars and lower the cost of getting around. People no longer have to fear losing a bike to theft, or handle the maintenance that ownership demands.

I would not say it solves the deeper urban planning challenges, though. There are still inequalities in access across different parts of the city, and it is obvious that some stations hold far fewer bikes than others, or none at all — because people prefer to cycle downhill, which piles the bikes up at certain stations in the centre.

03 — Take a long-term systems approach

The scheme aims at making Oslo a more sustainable and liveable city, not at a short-term fix. It contributes to lasting behavioural and environmental change, offering people a sustainable option in everyday life. It has solid roots in the city and it is still growing: it encourages physical activity and makes active transport far easier. Its success depends on continued investment in infrastructure, maintenance and expansion as Oslo gets bigger.

04 — Test and refine the design

Since opening in 2003 with around 1,000 bikes across 100 stations, the scheme has grown massively. In 2016 it was upgraded to a new, modern system with app-based unlocking, and the old set-up was replaced completely. The number of stations, bicycles, digital features and the service coverage have all been adjusted against user needs and city development, so it is genuinely improved through feedback and real-world use.

The gap I still see is breadth: it could be improved further with wider feedback on accessibility for the elderly, for tourists, and for people with disabilities.

05 — Work with the community, not for them

The service is designed around the needs of me and the rest of the community in Oslo, and its success rests on how we use the bikes and move through the city’s transport network. Our behaviour as users, along with our demands and feedback, has shaped where stations are placed and how the service has improved. Community involvement could still go further — letting residents have a bigger say in where stations land, or in how the service should work in the parts of the city it currently overlooks.

Conclusion

Having lived in Oslo for many years already, with more to come, I can tell that the city demonstrates how a clean, sustainable and simple idea can have a meaningful impact across an entire city. The service still has room to improve, but it helps people move around in a more sustainable, affordable and healthy way. Its good design shows in the fact that it solves practical problems and improves everyday life for me and the rest of the community in the core of Oslo.
